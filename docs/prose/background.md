In 2011, the City of San Diego implemented, for the first time, a nine-district plan with single-member district representation ([2012 San Diego elections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2012_San_Diego_elections)). The city has kept this structure of nine districts and nine councilmembers. In 2021, it redrew its district boundaries using data from the [2020 Census](https://www.kpbs.org/news/midday-edition/2021/12/16/san-diego-finalizes-new-map-of-city-council-districts). Redistricting is carried out by the city's independent [Redistricting Commission](https://www.sandiego.gov/redistricting-commission), which was created by the [City Charter](https://docs.sandiego.gov/citycharter/Article%20II.pdf) in 1992.

San Diego also uses staggered elections on a four-year cycle: even-numbered districts (2, 4, 6, 8) and odd-numbered districts (1, 3, 5, 7, 9) are elected in alternating election years. The city uses a two-round system, with a primary in June followed by a November runoff between the top two candidates in each district.

Given this system, it is worth analyzing how proportionality for minority communities could be improved. White and Hispanic voters appear to achieve at least proportional representation regularly. The Asian population appears to be growing, yet it remains consistently underrepresented in city government. Black voters have seen consistent representation of their preferred candidates over time with one candidate, primarily in District 4.

This report uses new and updated tools, such as [GerryChain](https://github.com/mggg/GerryChain) and [VoteKit](https://github.com/mggg/VoteKit), to simulate a variety of electoral systems and scenarios. Primarily, we examine simulations of multi-member districts elected by [Single Transferable Vote (STV)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_transferable_vote), low target-bloc turnout, and Cambridge ballot truncation.
